01 · Demand

A huge market, growing slowly

Demand is not the problem. Capturing it is — growth is ~2% a year, and incumbents hold the reviews, rankings, and relationships.

Details — where the work is, and why it's spoken for

The work concentrates in MCO airport transfers, Port Canaveral cruise transfers (including "land-and-sea" packages pairing a sailing with park days), Disney/Universal resort transfers, and convention and wedding work. Universal Epic Universe added ~4M visits in its partial first year, and Orlando's 132,685 hotel rooms ran at 71.4% occupancy.

But bookings flow through Google results, review counts, hotel concierges, travel agents, and cruise-line channels — all of which reward incumbency. A new company starts with zero reviews in a ~2%-growth market, so nearly every trip it wins must be taken from someone else. A huge market is not a capturable market.

02 · Your fleet

Three Traverses — the wrong tool for premium fares

Two 2018s and a 2024, all standard editions. Owning them kills the biggest start-up cost — but a Traverse is a midsize crossover, not the full-size SUV the premium prices assume.

Details — how the market prices this class

Majestic Private Transportation charges $110 for its GMC Acadia (the Traverse's sibling) for MCO–Disney, versus $120 for a Yukon XL / Suburban. That class gap — not effort or service quality — is what caps this fleet's blended fare at $115–$135.

One small offset: fuel. A Traverse returns ~19–20 mpg against ~15–16 for a Suburban-class SUV, which is why the model credits direct costs at $44/trip. It saves a couple of dollars a trip. It does not close the fare gap.

04 · Unit economics

One trip, honestly costed, loses money

Two rules drive everything: the driver's time is costed at $25/hr (a trip takes ~3 hours all-in), and owned vehicles still wear out.

Anatomy of one trip — fare in vs. costs out

Fare in (Base, Year 1)$122
Costs out$148 — loss of $26
Driver time $75 (3 hrs @ $25/hr)
Direct $44 (fuel, tolls, parking, cleaning)
Wear reserve $25 (raised — two vehicles are 8 years old)
Card fees ≈ $4 (2.9%)

That's before insurance, permits, or software. The margin operators quote in this business is mostly unpaid labor.

The structural finding

Break-even fare ≈ $148. Traverse fare ceiling ≈ $135. The gap isn't marketing or volume — at $135 the trip still loses ≈ $13, and at the realistic $122 it loses ≈ $26.

Slide to $148 and you reach ≈ $0. Slide to $135 — the top of what this class charges — and you're still at ≈ −$13.

Details — fixed costs don't care that the vehicles are paid off

Commercial livery insurance runs ≈ $7,000 per vehicle per year — insuring all three Traverses is ~$21,000 a year before a single trip is sold. Add City of Orlando and airport/port permits, booking and dispatch software, and payment fees. Fixed cost per vehicle is the bet, which is why the pilot places it once, not three times. Deadhead — the empty return leg — is the other margin killer: round-trips and packaged cruise work soften the loss per dollar earned, but at Traverse fares they don't erase it.

Details — assumptions, and the fare climb that isn't available
  • Blended fares: Base $122 → $128 → $132 (within the $115–$135 Traverse range). Volume ramps with seasonal slumps, not a flat trips × 52.
  • Costs: driver $75/trip · direct $44/trip · wear reserve $25/trip · insurance $7,000/vehicle/yr · card fees 2.9%.
  • A positive Year 3 would need the blended fare to climb toward $190 — premium black-car money. That climb is not available to this fleet: the Traverse isn't black-car eligible at any model year. The upside requires a different vehicle class, not better execution.
  • Paid ads don't rescue it: $6,000 of Google Ads buys ~60 bookings (~$100 each) against a −$26 contribution. Volume must come from referrals.
  • The only break-even that exists is a cash one for a single owner-driven vehicle — ~6–7 trips/week at $120–$125 — and it works only because the owner's labor goes unpaid.

07 · Requirements

Permits before trip one

Regulated at city, airport, port, and state level — and every permitted vehicle carries its full fixed cost whether it turns a wheel or not.

  • City of Orlando Vehicle-for-Hire permits — company, each driver, each vehicle.
  • GOAA / MCO permit — for pre-arranged airport pickups.
  • Port Canaveral permit — for cruise-terminal work.
  • Florida for-hire insurance minimums — $125k / $250k / $50k liability; ≈ $7,000/vehicle/yr in practice.
  • Sunbiz LLC + local business tax receipt.
  • Annual vehicle inspections and driver background checks.

08 · The defensible entry

A 90-day pilot: one vehicle, one question

Can referrals deliver 8+ trips a week at Traverse fares — without buying bookings with ads? The pilot answers that for ~$4,500–$6,500, with "no" kept affordable.

Days 1–30 · Set up
Insure & permit the 2024 only

The 2018s stay personal. Fixed-cost exposure is one vehicle, not three.

Days 31–60 · Sell
Win 2–3 referral sources

A vacation-rental manager, a travel agent, a wedding planner. Sell families of 4–5, round-trips, land-and-sea packages.

Days 61–90 · Measure
Track the real numbers

Paid trips, average fare, deadhead miles, your own hours — weekly, honestly.

The gate at day 90

8+ paid trips/week at a $120+ average fare, without paid ads — or stop.

Cash break-even for one owner-driven vehicle is ~6–7 trips/week at $120–$125, so the gate sits above it: clearing it means real demand, not just unpaid labor covering costs. Missing it costs a few thousand to learn — not $20,000+ insuring three vehicles.

Details — what passing the gate does and doesn't justify
  • It proves referral demand only. It does not change Traverse arithmetic — the ~$148 break-even still sits above this fleet's ceiling.
  • It justifies a fresh, separate decision: modelling a premium-class vehicle purchase (late-model Suburban-class, able to charge $190+ and clear the black-car tier) against documented, repeat referral demand.
  • It does not justify insuring the 2018s as front-line vehicles. They stay backup/overflow — their insurance adds fixed cost without adding fare power.
  • Even cheaper first test: overflow/affiliate driving for an established operator — they carry insurance, permits, and bookings while you learn the routes and the real hours, paid per trip.

09 · Risks

What could still go wrong

  • HIGHNegative contribution per trip. The model's central fact, not a tail risk: −$13 to −$26 per trip before fixed costs, with no fix inside this vehicle class.
  • HIGHHead-on UberXL competition. Your vehicle class is summonable on demand, with no booking friction. The private-transfer premium must be re-earned on every trip.
Details — further risks
  • Price ceiling pressure — rideshare surge and Mears' shuttle fares cap what families will pay; softness lands on an already-negative margin.
  • Discovery dependency — bookings concentrate in Google results and reviews; a slow review ramp, or one bad early review, hits a new entrant hardest.
  • Single-driver fragility — in the pilot shape, the business is the owner. Illness or one vehicle in the shop is a revenue stop.
  • Aging-fleet downtime — two 2018s at commercial mileage will fail; a breakdown on a cruise day damages the referral relationships the plan depends on.
  • Insurance repricing — $7,000/vehicle is a planning estimate; get a real quote before any other spending.
  • Demand shocks & regulatory change — hurricanes, airline or cruise schedule changes, and airport/port permit terms can all move the cost structure quickly.

Sources & method

  • Market figures: published 2025 statistics for Orlando visitation, MCO passenger traffic, Port Canaveral FY2025 movements, Orange County Convention Center attendance, and Orlando hotel inventory/occupancy.
  • Competitor details and fares: the operators' own published websites and rate information (Orlando Limo Tours, Elite Transportation, American Elite Transportation, Mears Connect), and published rideshare fare ranges for the MCO–Disney corridor.
  • Vehicle figures: manufacturer cargo specifications for the Chevrolet Traverse (23.0 cu ft, 2018; 22.9 cu ft, 2024) and Suburban (~41.5 cu ft) behind the third row; rideshare vehicle-eligibility rules for Uber Black.
  • The financial model is illustrative, built from the stated assumptions — it is not any operator's actual accounts. Its most sensitive input is commercial insurance: the $7,000-per-vehicle figure is a planning estimate and should be replaced with a real quote before any decision is made.

Verdict restated: 4/10 — Proceed with caution; the only defensible step is a 90-day, one-vehicle pilot on the 2024 Traverse with a pre-set kill line.
